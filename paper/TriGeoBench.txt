Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models show
strong vision–language capabilities, yet their
geometry-centric reasoning remains insuffi-
ciently understood.
Existing mathematical
benchmarks typically rely on rasterized fig-
ures and cover limited geometric knowledge
areas and response formats, making it dif-
ficult to assess whether models reason over
geometric structures or exploit superficial vi-
sual patterns. We introduce TriGeoBench, a
geometry-centric multimodal benchmark con-
structed from Chinese middle- and high-school
mathematics examinations. TriGeoBench con-
tains 7,000 question–answer pairs and 12,235
geometric figures with TikZ code, spanning
plane geometry, solid geometry, analytic ge-
ometry, function, and vector geometry across
diverse response formats.
Each instance
forms a {question–answer pair, image, TikZ}
triplet: question–answer pairs are stored in
LATEX, images as RGB figures, and TikZ pre-
serves geometric topology, symbolic annota-
tions, and relational structure. We evaluate rep-
resentative open- and closed-source MLLMs
through image-to-TikZ and multimodal rea-
soning probes. Results reveal persistent fine-
grained structure and image–text alignment
challenges. Oracle experiments with human-
curated reference TikZ suggest that accurate
executable representations provide complemen-
tary geometric cues; they do not evaluate an
end-to-end conversion pipeline. TriGeoBench
offers a testbed for structure-aware mathemati-
cal reasoning.

ures. Unlike text-only evaluation (Cobbe et al.,
2021; Hendrycks et al., 2021a; Gao et al., 2024),
these benchmarks examine diagram interpretation,
symbol–figure alignment, and the integration of
multiple information sources for mathematical
derivation. However, geometry-centric evaluation
retains two limitations: (1) Lack of precise struc-

mine whether models reason over precise structures
or correctly map mathematical symbols to diagram-
matic entities. (2) Limited coverage of geometric
knowledge areas and problem formats. Existing
datasets often cover only subsets of geometry and
restricted question types, with limited plane and
solid geometry or response-format diversity. They
therefore provide a coarse view of reasoning.
To address these limitations, we introduce Tri-
GeoBench, a geometry-centric multimodal bench-
mark for mathematical reasoning and structure-
aware diagram understanding. It is constructed
from public Chinese middle- and high-school ex-
amination problems and solution materials that re-
flect authentic pedagogical distributions. Manual
transcription, figure authoring, and independent hu-
man verification yield 7,000 question–answer pairs
and 12,235 high-quality figures with corresponding
TikZ code. The dataset spans plane geometry, solid
geometry, analytic geometry, function, and vector
geometry, and includes single-choice, fill-in-the-
blank, proof, and essay questions. Each statement
is represented in LATEX and each figure as editable
TikZ, preserving topology, symbolic annotations,
and relational structure more faithfully than raster
images alone. Figure 1 presents representative ex-
amples across difficulty levels.
We evaluate TriGeoBench through unified
image-to-TikZ and multimodal reasoning protocols
for representative general-purpose MLLMs. Cur-
rent models still struggle to recover fine-grained
relations, annotations, and spatial constraints from
figures. For reasoning, we compare text, raster
figures, human-curated reference TikZ, and their
combination. The reference-code condition is an
oracle diagnostic of whether an accurate executable
representation is useful once available, rather than
an image-to-TikZ-to-reasoning system. Its descrip-
tive gains suggest complementary geometric cues,
while raw code can expose exact construction pa-
rameters. Our contributions are:
• TriGeoBench Benchmark.

tion problems, covering diverse knowledge

• Unified Representation. Each instance is a

• Broad Evaluation of General-Purpose

TriGeoBench
TriGeoBench comprises question–answer pairs, as-
sociated images, and corresponding TikZ code. It
evaluates whether models reason over structured
figures rather than superficial textual or visual cues.
3.1
Data Collection Principles
TriGeoBench is built around three principles: di-
versity, figure-grounded reasoning, and structural
representation. Here, figure-grounded means that
a diagram contains solution-relevant, nondecora-
tive geometric information used by the reference
solution or needed to disambiguate relations. It
does not imply strict unsolvability from text alone,
because redundant wording, learned priors, or prior
exposure may support a correct answer without
the figure. Each figure is paired with executable
TikZ code to preserve topology, annotations, and
geometric relations.
We focus on geometry problems from publicly
available Chinese middle- and high-school math-
ematics exams and their corresponding solution
materials from the past three years. These sources
reflect authentic pedagogical distributions, enforce
standardized mathematical notation, and exhibit
naturally coupled text–figure reasoning patterns.
As a result, the dataset is well suited not only for
evaluating multimodal mathematical reasoning, but
also for supporting auxiliary tasks such as image-
to-TikZ. Tab. 1 presents a detailed comparison be-
tween TriGeoBench and existing benchmarks.
3.2
Annotation
TriGeoBench is constructed through a three-stage
annotation pipeline: source collection and prepro-
cessing, manual conversion to symbolic representa-
tions, and independent human verification.
Collection and preprocessing. We collect pub-
licly available Chinese mathematics examination
papers from online educational platforms, school
repositories, and open exam archives, covering
both junior and senior secondary education. These
materials typically contain question statements, an-
swer keys, and detailed solution analyses. We ex-
tract figure-containing problems related to our tar-
get knowledge areas and segment each candidate
into structured components, including the question
stem, answer, solution, and associated figure(s).
To reduce noise from heterogeneous layouts and
document formats, we normalize mathematical no-
tation, remove irrelevant document artifacts, and
align each question with its corresponding figure re-
gion. Annotators filter out problems without valid
figures, visually degraded samples, and highly re-
dundant templates, ensuring that retained instances
require meaningful text–figure interaction. We also
remove irrelevant metadata, personal information,
and source-specific formatting artifacts during pre-
processing. To reduce leakage and duplication, we
perform near-duplicate filtering within the corpus
and compare candidates with public geometry and
visual-math benchmarks when applicable. Because
the sources are public and proprietary pretraining
corpora are unknown, these checks reduce obvious
overlap but cannot rule out training-set contamina-
tion; details are in the appendices.
Manual conversion to LATEX and TikZ. To ob-
tain machine-usable and editable representations,
annotators manually transcribe each problem state-
ment into LATEX and author the corresponding TikZ
code using the original figure as reference. They
follow shared annotation guidelines to preserve
mathematical notation, problem semantics, geo-
metric topology, hidden edges, labels, incidence
relations, and relative positioning. Complex fig-
ures are iteratively compiled and compared with
the source diagram until their visual and structural
content is faithfully represented.
Independent verification and correction. Ev-
ery converted instance undergoes a separate human
review against the original source materials. Re-
viewers check the LATEX for semantic correctness
and the TikZ code for compilability, visual fidelity,
and structural consistency with the original figure.
In particular, they identify notation errors, missing
or distorted problem semantics, text–figure mis-
matches, and inaccurate geometric topology or la-
bel placement. Items with errors are returned for
correction, and figures are manually redrawn when
needed. This verification stage ensures that the final
dataset is syntactically standardized, semantically
faithful, and structurally aligned across question
text, figures, and TikZ representations. Additional
annotation details are provided in the appendices.
The annotation interface presents source images,
transcribed LATEX, and authored TikZ side by side,
with compilation checks during editing. The re-
view system records annotation status and accep-
tance decisions, tracks each instance through cor-
rection, and helps avoid duplicated or conflicting

3.3
Standardized Data Organization
To support downstream usage, we decouple the
textual and graphical components of each instance.
Specifically, the problem content is stored in LATEX
form, while each figure is stored separately in TikZ
form. In the text, the original figure locations are
replaced with explicit placeholders that specify the
figure identity and local position, with each place-
holder linked to the corresponding figure through
the question ID and figure index. The unified repre-
sentation enables flexible training and fine-tuning
for a range of downstream tasks. To further support
fine-grained evaluation and analysis, we categorize
TriGeoBench along three dimensions:
• Knowledge point. Each problem is labeled
with one or more knowledge areas, including
plane geometry, solid geometry, analytic ge-
ometry, function, and vector geometry. Since
many problems involve multiple topics, we
use both the question stem and the official
solution for model-assisted labeling, and man-
ually resolve ambiguous or overlapping cases.
Fig. 2 (a) shows the distribution across knowl-
edge areas.
• Problem type. We classify questions into four
response formats: single choice, fill-in-the-
blank, proof-based, and essay questions, with
their statistical distribution shown in Fig. 2 (b).
Initial labels are proposed by a large language
model based on the question form and answer
structure, and are subsequently verified by hu-
man annotators.
• Figure complexity and problem difficulty.
We annotate both dimensions in two stages.
First, K-means initializes three heuristic strata:
reference-solution length approximates prob-
lem difficulty, while TikZ-code length ap-
proximates figure complexity, as shown in
Fig. 2 (d). Second, experienced teachers and
educational experts calibrate the labels. They
adjust difficulty according to solution strategy,
inference depth, and text–figure interaction,
and refine complexity using labeled entities,
auxiliary constructions, geometric relations,
and spatial dependencies. These strata de-
scribe dataset-level difficulty and do not guar-
antee monotonic accuracy for every model.
Fig. 2 (c) shows the difficulty distribution.
After manual conversion and independent hu-
man verification, TriGeoBench comprises 7,000
question–answer pairs paired with 12,235 high-
quality geometric figures. Compared with prior
benchmarks, TriGeoBench offers broader topical
coverage, more diverse question formats, and na-
tive TikZ-based figure representations, making it
suited for both multimodal mathematical reasoning
and figure generation tasks.

Experiments
We design two tasks: Image-to-TikZ evaluates con-
version into editable code that recovers visual struc-
ture, geometric relations, and annotations; Mul-
timodal Mathematical Reasoning assesses joint
statement–diagram interpretation, symbol–figure
alignment, and answer derivation. Together, they
probe diagram generation and geometric reasoning.
Evaluation Models. We evaluate a broad spectrum
of MLLMs on TriGeoBench, including both open-
source and closed-source models. The open-source
set covers eight model families: Qwen3-VL mod-
els (Bai et al., 2025) of different scales, Qwen2.5-
VL (Bai Shuai et al., 2025), GLM-4.5V and GLM-
4.1V-9B-Thinking (Hong et al., 2025), Llama-3.2-
Vision (Meta, 2024), Gemma3 (Aishwarya Kamath
et al., 2025), LLaVA-OneVision (Li et al., 2024a),
and InternVL3 (Zhu et al., 2025). The closed-
source set includes four representative models:
Gemini-3.1-Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026), GPT-
5.4 (OpenAI, 2026), Seed-2.0-Pro (ByteDance
Seed Team, 2026), and Qwen3.5-Plus (Qwen Team,
2026).
Evaluation Metrics. For the Image-to-TikZ task,
we use four complementary metrics. Gen Acc mea-
sures whether the generated TikZ code can be suc-
cessfully compiled, evaluating executability. Im-
age Metrics assess visual consistency between ren-
dered predictions and ground-truth images. TikZ
Metrics compare generated and reference code to
quantify symbolic and structural similarity. Hu-
man Acc measures the proportion of rendered fig-
ures judged by humans as visually and mathemati-
cally faithful. For mathematical reasoning, we re-
port answer accuracy using an LLM-based grader,
counting each sub-question independently. Formal
definitions are provided in the appendices.
Evaluation Datasets. We construct the evaluation
set by sampling 1,000 instances from TriGeoBench
across different difficulty levels, including 500
Easy, 300 Medium, and 200 Hard problems. This
stratified sampling strategy ensures that the test set
covers a broad range of reasoning demands and
figure complexities. The remaining instances are
used as the training set for fine-tuning small-scale
multimodal large language models.
4.1
A Unified Image-to-TikZ Probe
Scope and Interpretation. All problem statements
and evaluation prompts are in Chinese, so scores
jointly reflect Chinese comprehension, mathemati-
cal reasoning, visual understanding, and structured-
input use; model comparisons are not language-
controlled estimates of geometry ability alone.
Text+TikZ uses human-curated reference code and
serves as an oracle diagnostic, not an end-to-end
conversion pipeline. We report descriptive point
estimates from a fixed run and do not treat small dif-
ferences as statistically conclusive. Public sources
and unknown proprietary pretraining corpora also
prevent a complete contamination guarantee.
Our goal is to probe general-purpose MLLMs
under a unified prompting protocol, not to establish
state-of-the-art performance against task-specific
systems. DeTikZify, AutomaTikZ, and TikZero
use distinct training corpora and task-specific opti-
mization; their direct evaluation on TriGeoBench
remains future work.

Inference and fine-tuning evaluation on small-
scale MLLMs. To further validate the effective-
ness and discriminative ability of TriGeoBench, we
conduct LoRA fine-tuning experiments on several
recent representative small-scale vision–language
models, with results reported in Tab. 3. For a fair
comparison, all models are fine-tuned under the
same LoRA configuration, including learning rate,
rank, and number of epochs. This controlled setup
enables a direct comparison of model adaptability
under the same training budget. The results on Gen
Acc show that LoRA fine-tuning does not consis-
tently improve all models. For instance, Qwen3-

5
Conclusion
TriGeoBench represents geometry problems as
aligned question–answer, image, and TikZ triplets.
Its unified probes expose limitations in structure
understanding and image–text alignment, while
8

oracle results with human-curated reference TikZ
suggest that accurate executable representations
can provide complementary geometric cues.
Limitations
TriGeoBench focuses on Chinese secondary-school
problems, so scores conflate Chinese compre-
hension, mathematical reasoning, vision, and
structured-input use and may not transfer to other
languages or curricula. Public sources and un-
known proprietary pretraining corpora prevent a
complete contamination guarantee; deduplication
only reduces obvious overlap. Reference TikZ may
expose exact construction parameters, and equiva-
lent programs complicate automatic evaluation. Fu-
ture work evaluates automatic conversion pipelines
and task-specific systems, expands the dataset’s
scope, and strengthens structure-aware evaluation.

A
Data Source, Licensing, and Leakage
Data sources. TriGeoBench is constructed from
publicly accessible Chinese junior- and senior-
secondary mathematics examination materials, in-
cluding online educational platforms, school repos-
itories, and open exam archives. The raw materials
typically contain question statements, answer keys,
solution analyses, and associated figures.
Dur-
ing preprocessing, we remove irrelevant metadata,
source-specific headers and footers, question in-
dices that do not affect problem semantics, and any
personal or institution-specific information when
present.
Licensing and release. Since the raw exami-
nation materials are collected from heterogeneous
public sources, their redistribution conditions may
vary. We therefore release the processed dataset
in a form that follows the applicable licensing
constraints of the original sources.
When nec-
essary, we provide derived annotations, normal-
ized LATEX question–answer pairs, rendered figures,
TikZ code, and metadata, while avoiding redistri-
bution of source documents that are not explicitly
licensed for reuse. The final release includes a data
statement describing source categories, preprocess-
ing steps, and usage restrictions.
Deduplication and leakage control. To reduce
duplicate or near-duplicate samples, we conduct
filtering over the collected corpus using textual
similarity and manual inspection. Problems based
on highly redundant templates are removed during
annotation. We further check candidate samples
against representative public geometry and multi-
modal mathematical reasoning benchmarks when
applicable. However, because some large multi-
modal models may have been trained on broad
web-scale data, we cannot fully rule out prior expo-
sure to public examination problems. We therefore
report the dataset construction procedure transpar-
ently and encourage future evaluations to consider
potential training-data contamination when inter-
preting results.
Privacy and sensitive information. The dataset
is built from educational mathematics problems
rather than personal records. We remove names,
school-specific metadata, document headers, and
11

other irrelevant identifying information when they
appear in source documents. The retained content
focuses on mathematical problem statements, solu-
tions, figures, and structured annotations.

B.1
Collection and Preprocessing
We collect publicly available Chinese mathematics
examination papers from online educational plat-
forms, school repositories, and open exam archives.
The collected materials cover both junior and senior
secondary education and typically include ques-
tion statements, answer keys, and detailed solution
analyses. These materials provide both the prob-
lem context and the intended reasoning process,
which helps verify whether extracted figures con-
tain solution-relevant, nondecorative information.
From the raw materials, we extract candidate
problems that contain figures and are relevant to
one or more target knowledge areas, including
plane geometry, solid geometry, analytic geometry,
functions, and vector geometry. Each candidate
problem is segmented into structured components,
including the question stem, answer, solution, and
associated figure(s). Due to heterogeneous layouts
and document styles, we perform careful normal-
ization, including cleaning line breaks, removing
question indices, deleting irrelevant headers and
footers, standardizing mathematical notation, and
aligning each question with its corresponding fig-
ure region.
After preprocessing, annotators screen the can-
didate pool by removing questions without valid
figures, cases with severely degraded visual quality,
and problems based on clearly redundant templates.
This filtering step improves the quality and diver-
sity of the dataset while ensuring that the retained
instances require meaningful text–figure reason-
ing rather than relying only on textual shortcuts or
superficial visual patterns.
B.2
Manual Conversion to LATEX and TikZ
To obtain unified and editable representations, hu-
man annotators manually transcribe each problem
statement into LATEX and author the corresponding
figure in TikZ using the original source as refer-
ence. Shared annotation guidelines require anno-
tators to preserve mathematical notation and prob-
lem semantics while producing complete, compil-
able code. The full guidelines are provided in the
“Guidelines for Manual Image-to-TikZ Annotation”
section below.
Manual TikZ authoring is particularly demand-
ing because complex figures require precise topol-
ogy, geometric incidence, symbolic labels, hid-
den edges, auxiliary lines, and relative positioning.
Annotators therefore compile and compare each
representation with the original figure throughout
the authoring process, with particular attention to
solid-geometry diagrams, function plots with crit-
ical points, and figures involving multiple nested
geometric relations.
B.3
Independent Verification and Correction
For every manually converted instance, an inde-
pendent reviewer compares the transcribed LATEX
and authored TikZ code with the original source
materials. The verification process focuses on four
recurring categories of errors:
1. Mathematical notation errors. These in-
clude malformed fractions, missing operators,
incorrect symbols, and wrong subscripts or
superscripts.
2. Semantic omissions or distortions. These
include missing conditions, incomplete solu-
tion steps, incorrect answer transcription, or
changes in the meaning of the original prob-
lem.
3. Text–figure inconsistencies. These include
mismatched point labels, missing geometric
objects, inconsistent references between the
question and the figure, or incorrect associa-
tion between a figure and its problem state-
ment.
4. TikZ structural errors. These include in-
correct topology, missing auxiliary lines, in-
accurate relative positions, misplaced labels,
missing hidden edges, and incorrect rendering
of geometric or functional relations.
Items that do not pass review are returned to the
annotators for correction or complete redrawing
when necessary. The final TikZ code must be com-
pilable and preserve the mathematical intent and
visual reasoning structure of the original figure.

system, as illustrated in Figure 5.

We evaluate the generated TikZ/LATEX outputs from
four complementary perspectives: renderability,
visual fidelity, code-level similarity, and human-
verified correctness. In addition, for the mathe-
matical reasoning task, we report answer accuracy
based on automatic grading by an LLM judge.

C.7
Quality Control
To ensure consistency across modalities, each final-
ized instance is checked from three perspectives.
First, the problem text, answer, and solution must
preserve the semantics of the original source. Sec-
ond, the rendered figure from the TikZ code must
match the original figure in terms of key geometric
objects, labels, topology, and relative spatial rela-
tions. Third, textual references in the problem must
be aligned with the corresponding visual entities
in the figure. These checks ensure that each data
instance forms a coherent {LATEX question–answer
pair, image, TikZ} triplet suitable for both image-
to-TikZ generation and multimodal mathematical
reasoning evaluation.
D
Guidelines for Manual Image-to-TikZ
Annotation
Human annotators follow a common checklist
when converting mathematical figures into struc-
tured symbolic representations. The checklist em-
phasizes identification of core visual elements and
faithful, compilable LATEX/TikZ authoring.
Manual Image-to-TikZ Annotation Check-
list
Inspect the source problem image and record
the core visual information in the mathemat-
ical and geometric domains, including the
coordinate-system type, function expressions,
graphical boundaries, coordinates of special
points, auxiliary lines, filled regions, and leg-
end descriptions. Then manually author clear
academic-style LATEX drawing code.
The requirements are as follows:
1. Prefer the TikZ package to ensure the clar-
ity of vector graphics.
2. Strictly follow the parameter specifica-
tions in the original figure and do not omit
any important details.
3. Keep the code structure clean and well
organized, with detailed comments to fa-
cilitate subsequent parameter adjustment.
4. Provide a brief explanation indicating
how the key parts of the code correspond
to the visual elements in the original fig-
ure.
E
Generated Token Analysis
Figure 6 reports the average number of generated
tokens across difficulty levels and input formats.
The number of output tokens generally increases
with problem difficulty, indicating that harder ge-
ometric problems tend to require longer reason-
ing chains. In contrast, introducing additional in-
put modalities, such as figures or TikZ, does not
substantially increase the number of generated to-
kens. This suggests that the performance changes
observed in the main experiments are mainly as-
sociated with differences in available geometric
information and multimodal fusion ability, rather
than with a systematic increase in generation length
or reasoning cost.

Reliability Analysis of LLM-based
Evaluation
To assess the LLM-based evaluator, we conduct a
human-audit agreement analysis on 300 sampled
responses: 78 choice, 84 fill-in, 53 proof, and 85
solution items. Human annotators independently
score each response using the same criteria as the
LLM judge, and we compare the scores after nor-
malization by each problem’s maximum score.
Figure 7a reports Pearson r = 0.629, Spearman
ρ = 0.607, and normalized-score MAE = 0.062.
Exact agreement is 91.7% and within-one-point
agreement is 99.0%. Figure 7b further breaks down
agreement by question type. The point estimates
are higher for objective formats than for proof-

Overall, this audit supports the fixed grader for
